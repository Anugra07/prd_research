# Business / Market Research Sources

Starting points for the business track (Phases B2-B5). Prefer primary, independent, dated sources over marketing. Always record the capture date for anything that changes (pricing, rankings, funding).

## Competitor and product intelligence

- **Pricing / packaging:** the competitor's own pricing page (capture the date - pricing changes). Archive via the Wayback Machine (https://web.archive.org) to compare over time.
- **Positioning / messaging:** competitor home page and "why us" pages - read for the segment and promise they target, not for facts.
- **Independent reviews:** G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Gartner Peer Insights. Read the critical reviews and the "switched from / switched to" notes - that's where real differentiation and churn reasons show.
- **App stores:** App Store / Google Play rankings, ratings, review themes (for consumer/mobile). Data.ai / Sensor Tower for estimates (label "estimated").
- **Launch / community signal:** Product Hunt (launch reception), Hacker News (Show HN, comment sentiment), relevant subreddits, Discord/Slack communities.

## Traction and company signals

- **Funding / size:** Crunchbase, PitchBook (if available), press releases (treat as vendor-sourced). Funding is an input signal, not proof of product quality.
- **Hiring as a roadmap signal:** the competitor's careers page and LinkedIn job posts reveal where they're investing (e.g. hiring ML infra, or a sales team -> moving upmarket).
- **Public companies:** 10-K / 10-Q / S-1 filings (SEC EDGAR), earnings call transcripts (Motley Fool, Seeking Alpha) - the most honest source on a public competitor's metrics, segments, and stated risks.
- **Web/traffic estimates:** Similarweb, Ahrefs (label all "estimated").

## Market sizing and trends (always label "estimated", show method)

- **Bottom-up sizing** (preferred): (# target accounts) x (realistic ACV) x (reachable share). Always sanity-check any top-down number against this.
- **Top-down references:** analyst summaries (Gartner/Forrester/IDC press snippets), industry association reports, government statistics. Cite the year; markets move.
- **Demand signals:** Google Trends, search-volume tools, public job-posting counts, GitHub stars trajectory for dev tools.

## Frameworks (use as lenses, cite when a claim leans on one)

- Jobs-to-be-Done (Christensen) - for the core "what is it hired to do".
- Value Proposition Canvas / positioning (April Dunford, "Obviously Awesome") - for UVP and category.
- Porter's Five Forces - for structural moat/defensibility.
- Pirate metrics (AARRR) - as a checklist for growth levers, not gospel.
- Crossing-the-chasm / segment focus - for sequencing expansion.

Frameworks organize thinking; they are not evidence. Every conclusion still needs a real source.

## How to evaluate a business source

1. Independent or self-interested? (Independent review vs. the vendor's own marketing or a press release.)
2. Dated and current? (Pricing, rankings, funding decay fast.)
3. Is there a number with a method, or only an adjective?
4. Sample bias? (Reviews skew to extremes; unicorn case studies ignore the failures.)
5. Comparable stage/segment to this product? (A PLG tactic from a $1B company may not fit a seed-stage tool.)
